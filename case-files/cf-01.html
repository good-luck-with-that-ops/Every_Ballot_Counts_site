What’s going on

Federal cyber law is written two ways in the same statute. The Homeland Security Secretary can act on a “reasonably suspected” incident. {D} Congress has to be told only seven days after an agency has “a reasonable basis to conclude” a major incident occurred. {D} Unauthorized access to the personal data of 100,000 or more people is a major incident automatically, and that makes it a “significant cyber incident” with the FBI leading the response. {D} The government has already put a suspicion in writing: a July 2026 DHS-CISA guide says China “breached multiple state voter registration systems.” {D}

Part two: the words travel. On Nov. 14 a Texas county party asked a federal judge to make every Texas county hand count the federal races and “correct” machine totals to match. Its amended complaint opens with sentences from Mullin’s Aug. 24 letter, stated as its own allegations: “Election security is national security.” {D} Counting runs of eight or more identical words, it reproduces 374 of the letter’s 758 words. {D} The letter’s “trust but verify” line runs back to an Aug. 2022 letter in which Russ Vought asked North Carolina to check its voter rolls against “all available federal and state databases.” {D}

Part three: fill in the blank. On Nov. 13, the afternoon of the FAA ground stop, the FBI warned that “specialized super intelligence (SI) agents” were being used against targets that include “government.” {D} The alert names no country, but its second note is Microsoft’s report on a “nation-state campaign” by “a China-state aligned actor.” {D} Three days later Mullin, the only official who can declare a significant incident, said of the outages: “I believe that it’s the SI.” {R} The government’s own definition of an “SI incident” covers “severe operational malfunctions resulting in harm,” with no attacker needed. {D} The opinion piece: Fill in the Blank.

Part four: maybe we’ll learn. After the FBI’s crime-records database was reported breached, the President said on Nov. 21, “Maybe we’ll learn about the voter fraud, all the voter fraud that took place.” {R} No official has said that database holds a single voter record. The same week, the officials who would shape the Dec. 18 report and any emergency changed: Stephen Miller pulled Mullin off TV and sent out DNI Jay Clayton, who said, “This is not a cyberattack,” {R} and the National Cyber Director was replaced by an acting director, Kirsten Davies. {R} The DHS Secretary can declare a “significant incident” only “in consultation with the National Cyber Director.” {D} The opinion piece: Maybe We’ll Learn (tee hee).

The emergency directive needs only a suspicion

44 U.S.C. 3553(h)(1)(A), Federal Information Security Modernization Act

The trigger is a “known or reasonably suspected” incident, and the reach includes “systems used or operated by another entity on behalf of an agency.” {D}

Why it matters. One signature from the DHS Secretary reaches contractor platforms too. {P}

What we presume

Presumed means it’s our inference from the pattern, and no source says it. We don’t extend the government a presumption of regularity, so we say plainly where we presume otherwise. A claim that a named person did something wrong never rests on this mark.

  • {P} One set of facts can open three doors: a major incident, a significant cyber incident and a “significant incident.”
  • {P} The last step is one sentence: that the 2020 breach reached the voter data the government holds now.
  • {P} We wouldn’t know the clock was running.
  • {P} The idea behind Mullin’s letter (check every voter against every database, and tie the grant money to it) was Vought’s ask in 2022, written into the 2023 plan, and built in 2025 and 2026 by people from his network. We haven’t found Vought’s name on the Texas case.
