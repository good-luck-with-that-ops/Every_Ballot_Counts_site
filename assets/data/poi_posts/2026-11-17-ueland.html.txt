<p><em>EBC &amp; [POI] Profiles: Eric Ueland. This is the first in a series on the people we're watching. Each person gets a profile page on <a href="https://everyballotcounts.io">everyballotcounts.io</a> that keeps updating from the record after the post goes stale.</em></p>
<p>On Monday, the president nominated Eric Ueland, Russ Vought's deputy at the Office of Management and Budget, to be Comptroller General of the United States, the head of the Government Accountability Office.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-1" href="#footnote-1">1</a> GAO is Congress's watchdog. It audits the federal government's books, decides whether the executive branch broke the law by refusing to spend money Congress appropriated, and can sue to make the government spend it. Considering that's exactly what got Trump impeached the first time (holding back Ukraine aid Congress had approved, which GAO found broke the law), I think a presumption of irregularity is pretty apt here.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-2" href="#footnote-2">2</a></p>
<p>Ueland has spent the past year as the acting head of USAID, running its "closeout," including a plan to hold back <strong>$19.2 billion</strong> in foreign aid that 17 senators call an illegal impoundment.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-3" href="#footnote-3">3</a> He has also been performing the duties of the official who prepares the government's financial statements, which GAO is required by law to audit.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-4" href="#footnote-4">4</a></p>
<p>So the guy who's been moving the money wants the job of deciding whether moving it was legal. If he's confirmed, he gets <strong>one 15-year term</strong>, and no president can fire him; the only ways out are impeachment or a joint resolution of Congress.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-5" href="#footnote-5">5</a> Confirmed this winter, he'd be there until roughly <strong>2041</strong>. (That date is our arithmetic, not a document.)</p>
<p>Even the people who've held this job are objecting. Today the last two Comptrollers General, Gene Dodaro and David Walker (both confirmed unanimously), wrote with Steve Hanke that "the Nov. 19, 2026, confirmation hearing should be canceled," because "the Commission and its members have never met."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-6" href="#footnote-6">6</a> They didn't even name him. They didn't need to. That commission got a Veterans Day afternoon request for names by 8 a.m. the next morning, and no Democratic names were submitted.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-7" href="#footnote-7">7</a></p>
<p>His own side isn't unanimous either. Punchbowl reports that Sen. John Curtis's office says the Utah Republican is worried the pick is too partisan.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-8" href="#footnote-8">8</a> And back in 2010, the last time anyone picked a Comptroller General, Susan Collins said the next one "must assume the responsibilities of the office with a completely nonpartisan attitude."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-9" href="#footnote-9">9</a></p>
<p>Republicans have 53 seats until January, and they want this done before Democrats take over.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-10" href="#footnote-10">10</a> If every Democrat votes no, it takes four Republicans to stop him. I can't believe we have to hope John Thune refusing to be professionally embarrassed is what saves our democracy, but I'll take what I can get.</p>
<h2>What he's done (the receipts)</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Holds six titles at once.</strong> His sworn financial disclosure lists OMB management deputy, acting OMB chief of staff, acting ethics chief, USAID deputy and then acting head, and "Senior Official Performing the Duties of Controller."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-11" href="#footnote-11">11</a></li>
<li><strong>Ran the USAID closeout.</strong> On April 20, 2026, Congress was notified of a plan to reserve <strong>$19,178,471,868</strong> for "close-out" costs. Seventeen senators wrote to him by name calling it an illegal impoundment.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-12" href="#footnote-12">12</a> When he briefed the House, Democrats wrote that he "failed to provide a mathematical justification for why $19.2 billion is needed."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-13" href="#footnote-13">13</a> (To be fair, the mass terminations happened before he got there. He inherited the wreck, then asked for $19 billion to tow it.)</li>
<li><strong>Co-signed the downsizing memo.</strong> OMB's M-26-03 told agencies to "Reduce the federal workforce by eliminating unnecessary positions," and his signature is right next to Vought's.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-14" href="#footnote-14">14</a></li>
<li><strong>Ran the ethics office, then left it empty.</strong> An OMB political appointee was running the government's ethics office. He left in December, the acting clock ran out, and the office had no leader.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-15" href="#footnote-15">15</a></li>
<li><strong>Chaired the inspectors general's council while OMB froze its money.</strong> As OMB's management deputy he was the council's statutory Executive Chair while OMB withheld about $28 million of its funding.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-16" href="#footnote-16">16</a></li>
<li><strong>Kept the books GAO couldn't sign off on.</strong> GAO's latest audit of the government's financial statements again couldn't express an opinion and singled out the "process for preparing the consolidated statements," which is the Controller's office.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-17" href="#footnote-17">17</a></li>
<li><strong>Told us his standard.</strong> His goal for management is driving the federal agenda "consistent with presidential direction and presidential intent."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-18" href="#footnote-18">18</a> Fine motto for OMB. Terrible one for Congress's watchdog.</li>
</ul>
<h2>What the job is</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>15 years, one term, can't be fired by the president.</strong><a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-19" href="#footnote-19">19</a> It was built to outlast any president. That's the point.</li>
<li><strong>Rules on impoundments, and can sue over them.</strong> GAO has found violations again and again this term, including twice that pocket rescissions were illegal.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-20" href="#footnote-20">20</a> Under the Impoundment Control Act, the Comptroller General alone decides whether to take the administration to court.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-21" href="#footnote-21">21</a> As one outlet put it: "If Ueland is confirmed, it could end any threat of a lawsuit."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-22" href="#footnote-22">22</a></li>
<li><strong>Audits the government's books and fights for records.</strong> That includes the statements prepared while he was acting Controller.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-23" href="#footnote-23">23</a></li>
<li><strong>Writes the audit rules every inspector general has to follow.</strong><a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-24" href="#footnote-24">24</a> Hold onto that one.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Why this one keeps me up at night</h2>
<p><strong>[P]</strong> The scariest version isn't a biased ruling. GAO's decisions don't bind anyone anyway; the last Comptroller General said "Congress is our enforcement authority."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-25" href="#footnote-25">25</a> The real power is in the lawsuit, the records fight and the audit signature, and all of those can be used by <em>doing nothing</em>. A Comptroller General who never files, never escalates and never looks too hard leaves no ruling to criticize, for 15 years. And this nominee's job for the past year has included the very things this office exists to check: he held back money senators call an illegal impoundment, kept books GAO couldn't certify, left the ethics office empty and chaired the IGs' council while his own office starved it.</p>
<p><strong>Then there's the data.</strong> A little-known exemption in the Inspector General Act says a computer match "performed by an Inspector General or by an agency in coordination with an Inspector General" isn't a "matching program" under the Privacy Act "or any other provision of law."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-26" href="#footnote-26">26</a> That switches off the written matching agreement, the 30-day notice before anyone's benefits get cut, and the Federal Register notice that tells the public a match exists at all. It also drops the clause guaranteeing "that the Comptroller General may have access to all records."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-27" href="#footnote-27">27</a> Meanwhile the IGs keep getting new seats at the table. The Vice President's fraud task force can add "inspectors general" and calls for "information and data sharing" with law enforcement.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-28" href="#footnote-28">28</a> A new North Carolina task force put seven IGs next to the FBI and HSI.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-29" href="#footnote-29">29</a> The IGs' own data platform already holds "more than 1.6 billion records."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-30" href="#footnote-30">30</a></p>
<p>Courts this year kept finding that the government's citizenship and data-sharing systems likely broke the Privacy Act, from the SAVE overhaul to bulk "likely noncitizen" voter lists.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-31" href="#footnote-31">31</a> Honest caveat: those cases turned on rules the IG exemption doesn't touch, and <strong>we haven't found a document showing a task force using it.</strong> <strong>[P]</strong> But it's a door. If benefit, tax, identity and immigration data start moving "in coordination with" an IG, the paperwork that let plaintiffs find SAVE might never exist. We're watching for the infrastructure going live: a data agreement or privacy assessment citing the exemption, or matching notices quietly disappearing while matching goes up. The person who sets the IGs' audit rules and decides how hard GAO looks would be the guy who chaired their council from inside OMB.</p>
<p><strong>And look who's on Thursday's panel with him.</strong><a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-32" href="#footnote-32">32</a> Michael Chamberlain, to run the ethics office Ueland left empty, who runs the conservative watchdog group Protect the Public's Trust.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-33" href="#footnote-33">33</a> Michael LoSapio, Palantir's chief information security officer, to run CISA, the agency in charge of election-infrastructure security.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-34" href="#footnote-34">34</a> And inspector general nominees including Dennis Kirk, a Project 2025 contributor, for Interior,<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-35" href="#footnote-35">35</a> and Mark Priebe for Education, who said in July that "the executive branch should have some say over their policies and their procedures."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-36" href="#footnote-36">36</a> <strong>[P]</strong> One lame-duck morning: the government's auditor, its ethics chief, its election-security chief and four inspectors general, all before the Senate flips. If we thought Stephen Miller as chief of staff was a middle finger to the voters, this is giving double birds off the back of a motorcycle with a cigarette hanging out of your mouth. (And a Palantir executive heading to CISA before January 3 doesn't exactly lower my blood pressure, but more to come on that once I have more research to go on than vibes.)</p>
<p>People who don't usually agree with me are saying it too. A former GAO senior lawyer says the process "violates both the letter and the spirit of the law."<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-37" href="#footnote-37">37</a> Jack Goldsmith asked: "Could he possibly be expected to switch teams and defend congressional prerogatives?"<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-38" href="#footnote-38">38</a></p>
<p>The hearing is Thursday.<a class="footnote-anchor" data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" id="footnote-anchor-39" href="#footnote-39">39</a> If your senator is a Republican, remind them this seat outlasts their majority by about fourteen years. <a href="https://5calls.org/issue/eric-ueland-gao-comptroller-general/">5 Calls has a script ready</a> if you'd rather not wing it.</p>
<p>Great plan, everybody. And once we've given him audit rights, let's hire Arthur Andersen to take another look at Enron. Just to make sure everything's all buttoned up over there.</p>
<p>— Amanda</p>
