Part four of "What's the BS, Ru$$?" Part one was the law. Part two was the copy/paste. Part three was the plan. This one's about the people carrying the plan ... and why you should never tell them a secret.

You can't tell this kid anything

You know when you tell a kid something's a secret, and for the next hour they stand there with their hands behind their back, swinging side to side, going "I know something you don't knowww"?

OK. Hold that picture.

On Friday night, CNN reported that someone broke into the FBI's National Crime Information Center, the database police everywhere use to share warrants and criminal records. It would be the second FBI system breached in recent months.1 A serious government says "we're investigating" and goes quiet.

Ours called into Fox News on Saturday morning. Here's the President of the United States, talking to his own former press secretary about the hack of his own FBI:

"Well, we just heard about it, and I will say it's not good. I don't like that."2

Fine! Normal! Then:

"I said, 'Well, that's an interesting situation. I wonder who's doing it, because wouldn't it be great to learn about Russia, Russia.'"3

"Maybe we'll learn things that we would have never learned otherwise."

"Maybe we'll learn about the voter fraud, all the voter fraud that took place."4

I wonder who's doing it. Tee hee.

Two things before we go on. One: that database is a crime index. It's "restricted to law enforcement use," nobody has said what was taken, and no official has said it holds a single voter record.5 He went to "voter fraud" anyway, eighteen days after his party lost both chambers of Congress.6 Two: this is the same guy who stood on a stage on Oct. 6 and said "We have some big surprises coming up in a few weeks, right Russ?"7

He cannot. Keep. A secret. And he'd just gotten home from a week away, to find out what the babysitter did with the other kids ...

The week the toys got taken away

In date order. He was in Manila and China for most of it.8

  • Mon., Nov. 16. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin goes on Hannity to explain the week's outages (flights, banks, Microsoft) and passes along a theory from a tech guy he knows: "I believe that it's the SI." (That's "super intelligence," the administration's new name for AI. He clarified that the tech guy was not Elon Musk.)9 White House group chats, per Politico: "WTF."10

  • Tues., Nov. 17. Stephen Miller, chief of staff for all of eight days, pulls Mullin off TV. From Manila. In the early morning hours. Politico: "Markwayne Mullin is on a primetime time-out." A person close to the White House: "We need to speak with one voice and the right voice, and the decision was made to put Jay out there."11 (An administration official says nobody's in trouble. They just "wanted to make sure that the top official with the best knowledge was the person who went out on each of these topics." Sure.)12

  • Same day. "Jay" is Jay Clayton, the Director of National Intelligence. On CNBC: "This is not a cyberattack, and this is not a coordinated effort by a nation-state."13 On Wednesday he says it again, to Congress: "They are not a coordinated or state-sponsored attack."14

  • Thurs., Nov. 19. The National Cyber Director, Sean Cairncross, is out. CBS: Miller "had long wanted Cairncross out of the job," set up the search and picked the replacement.15 One official told Fox he "did not want to serve another two years in the role." His boss told reporters he was fired "because I didn't think that person was good enough."16 Pick one. (They hired him. His job before this one was chief operating officer of the Republican National Committee.)17 At least he didn't need a pardon to leave. Low bar. Still a bar.

  • Fri., Nov. 20. Dad's home. On the outages: "They're all separate events." "And when they upgrade, bad things happen."18 Same day, he signs an order telling every federal contractor to tell the White House about any request from Congress and "abide by any instructions" on what to withhold.19

  • Sat., Nov. 21. "I wonder who's doing it."

So the official line all week, enforced from the other side of the Pacific: nothing happened, nobody did it, and (Clayton's actual words) "it's a good news story."20 Then the one person nobody can bench picks up the phone and says ooooh, maybe somebody did, and maybe we'll learn about the voter fraud.

Which brings us to the new kid.

Meet the new kid

Kirsten Davies, the Pentagon's chief information officer, is now the acting National Cyber Director. Here's everything the record says about her, and I mean everything:

  • Miller picked her (CBS, above).

  • She comes from Pete Hegseth's Pentagon. That's the shop that told the NSA, Cyber Command and two other intelligence agencies in September to "mobilize every authorized asset, capability, and partnership under your command" to defend elections.21 Hegseth says she'll "advance President Trump's America First agenda across the cybersecurity landscape."22

  • Her own first words in the job: "Our opportunity is to dominate the cyber landscape for generations to come."23

  • She's acting. The job needs Senate confirmation, and it's a lame duck.24

  • The Washington Sun reports that this spring, while she ran Pentagon IT, she bought stock in three companies with Pentagon business, despite an ethics agreement promising to avoid conflicts. A watchdog group's verdict: "a clear conflict of interest."25

That's the list. I haven't found one document tying her to any election work, and we looked. [P] What I have is who chose her, when, and what the chair does.

And what the chair does is this: by law, the DHS Secretary declares a "significant incident" only "in consultation with the National Cyber Director."26 (If you're new here, that declaration is the trigger this whole series is about.)

So count the chairs.

Three chairs, one date

The worst case needs exactly three people to get started. The DNI writes the report. The DHS Secretary signs the declaration. The cyber director gets consulted. In one week, Miller handed the first one the microphone, took the second one's away, and replaced the third. [P]

The report has a due date. Under a 2018 executive order, the DNI owes an assessment of foreign interference "Not later than 45 days after the conclusion of" the election.27 That's about Dec. 18. Then the Attorney General and the DHS Secretary get 45 more days to say whether any of it "materially affected the security or integrity" of the election's infrastructure.

Here's what people miss: the Dec. 18 report goes to the President. Nothing makes it public. (The follow-on report for the 2022 midterms was finished in February 2023 and released that December.)28 We see whatever he decides to show us.

The kid decides whether to open his hands. So what could be in them?

What could be in the box

My ranking, by what the record supports. All of it is [P].

Most likely: nothing. Or one line. A post. A "very bad things in there" from the podium. The man writing the report spent this week telling Congress nobody attacked anything.

Likely: the cherry-picked summary. "Foreign actors targeted ..." with the old China claims reheated. That one's already on White House letterhead: China "obtained the personal data of 220 million American voters."29 This version doesn't blow anything up. It sells the next thing: another proof-of-citizenship push, a 2028 order, a reason the new House shouldn't get the briefing. The fan fiction is already written, too. American Thinker has the date ("No later than December 18th") and the payoff: "the pretext will be created to declare a national emergency around the 2028 election."30

Possible: "tainted." One sentence saying a foreign actor accessed a federal system that holds voter or citizenship data. That's the trigger from Part three. This month gave us two federal breaches that reached the public through leaks, not announcements,31 and a President who tied one of them to voter fraud all by himself.

The tail: the whole thing. I promised you one fully baked hypothetical. Here it is.

OK, the worst one. All the way.

This is a worst-case hypothetical, not a prediction. "Legal" here means the argument they'd make, not one a court would buy. For each step: who has to actually do something, what they've already said, and what's in the way.

Step 0. Now through Dec. 17. Kash Patel's FBI.
Keep the hack and the fraud hunt open side by side. When a federal cyber incident is big enough, the FBI leads the response, which means the FBI holds the evidence.32 Already said: Patel claimed in April that the FBI had proof 2020 was stolen and that arrests were coming. Neither showed up.33 And the FBI and Dan Bishop, DOJ's election-fraud prosecutor, just spent more than two hours questioning the man who ran Fulton County's 2020 election.34 In the way: a crime database isn't a voter roll.

Step 1. Dec. 18. Jay Clayton.
The report says a foreign-linked actor got into systems holding voter or citizenship data. Some of his raw material would come from Hegseth's agencies, under orders since September to "execute collection and production on foreign threats to our elections."35 Already said: "This is not a cyberattack." In the way: his own mouth. (Fine print: he was talking about the outages, not the election. That's a door, and it's open.)

Step 2. Dec. 18-21. Markwayne Mullin, after a chat with Kirsten Davies.
He declares a "significant incident." It only has to be "likely to occur imminently," the harm can be to "public confidence," and he "may not delegate" it to anyone.36 It runs up to 120 days and he can renew it. From Dec. 18, that's mid-April ... well past Jan. 3, when the new Congress is seated. Already said: "After this election we'll be gathering all the data, we'll be gathering the voting logs."37 His boss supplied the public-confidence part on Nov. 9: "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest."38 In the way: it has to go in the Federal Register within 72 hours. He's benched from TV, but you don't need a camera to sign something.

Step 3. Dec. 21-24. Pete Hegseth.
A declared significant incident unlocks a law that lets the Secretary of Defense order reservists onto active duty without their consent.39 Already said: elections are "a no-fail mission."40 In the way: the same law says those reservists can't be used for Insurrection Act work. It puts people in uniform on duty. It doesn't let them police anybody. Yet.

Step 4. Dec. 22-28. Todd Blanche's DOJ.
"Secure the evidence." Demands, subpoenas and warrants for voter rolls, logs and ballots, under the records law DOJ already cited to election officials in all 50 states.41 The FBI taking Fulton County's 2020 ballots in January is the dress rehearsal.42 Already said: Blanche, Nov. 12: "We are actively, and will continue to investigate election integrity cases criminally and civilly, and that wasn't expected to start or stop — no matter what the results of the election were last week."43 His department, in the Alaska Senate case: "preservation and segregation of potentially tainted ballots."44 A federal prosecutor in Washington state, on the radio, as quoted in a judge's order: "If we want to cure the issue, we may just have to cut the state out."45 In the way: judges. As of Nov. 10, DOJ had lost 28 of its voter-roll lawsuits.46

Step 5. Dec. 26-30. Everybody else.
States say no. A judge says no. People show up outside with signs. It's the one step the other side has to supply, and in this script, somebody saying no is the whole point. Already said: Miller's phrase for a judge ruling against the President is "legal insurrection."47 Mullin's testimony this week lists "anarchists and anti-fascists" and "anti-Americanism" under domestic violent extremism.48 In the way: it takes actual obstruction. A court order isn't one. A protest isn't one.

Step 6. Dec. 29-31. Stephen Miller and Russ Vought.
The paperwork, which is the part that's already done. NBC reported last fall that officials "have drafted legal defenses and various options for invoking the act," and called Miller "a leading and longtime proponent of invoking the Insurrection Act."49 His old deputy now has his old job as homeland security adviser.50 Already said: Russ, on why his think tank was building the legal case: "We want to be able to shut down the riots and not have the legal community or the defense community come in."51 Politico reported that his shop listed "invoking the Insurrection Act on Day One" as a priority; its spokesperson called the reporting "false."52 (Not Project 2025, for once. The Act isn't in the book. This one's Russ's own shop.) In the way: in 2025 the White House staff secretary wrote a memo against invoking it, back when Susie Wiles was chief of staff. Miller has her job now.53

Step 7. Jan. 1. The President.
First, a proclamation ordering "the insurgents to disperse and retire peaceably to their abodes."54 Then the order itself, on the theory that "unlawful obstructions, combinations, or assemblages" have made it "impracticable to enforce the laws of the United States in any State by the ordinary course of judicial proceedings."55 Already said: "I will institute the INSURRECTION ACT, which many Presidents have done before me." And: "Do you know that I could use that immediately and no judge can even challenge you on that."56 Asked by TIME in September whether he'd rule out declaring an emergency around the midterms: "I don't rule anything out or in. We'll see."57 In the way: this law has "rarely" been tested in court. That cuts both ways.

Why Jan. 1? [P] It's a holiday. The old Congress is out the door, the new one isn't sworn in until the 3rd, and half the country is asleep on a couch. Two days isn't long. It's the two days nobody's at their desk.

Now the paragraph that keeps me honest. Even at step 7, none of this un-certifies an election, takes a ballot without a warrant, or decides who gets sworn in. The House judges its own elections, and the Speaker says he won't block anyone.58 Troops don't change a result. What changes things is a sentence: there is a declared emergency and an active federal investigation into foreign tampering with these results. That's what an objection on Jan. 3 would quote. [P]

And the chain is weakest right where you'd hope. Step 1 needs Clayton to eat his words. Step 5 needs somebody else to break something. And Miller spent this whole week enforcing "nothing happened." Either that sinks the theory ... or the report is the one place the story is allowed to change. [P]

Or.

Or they're just kids

He put voter fraud in the same breath as the "Russia, Russia, Russia hoax" and the "Ukraine, Ukraine, Ukraine, Ukraine hoax."59 (Four Ukraines. I counted.) That's a man reading his grievances off the back of his hand.

So here's the version I'd actually bet on. Dec. 18 comes. There's a post, maybe a shrug. The acting cyber chief stays acting. The contractor order quietly keeps the new House from asking the vendors what happened this month. And the data pipe from Part three is sitting right where we left it, ready for 2028. [P]

I know "they're unserious" is supposed to be the comforting version. I don't find it comforting. A kid who can't keep a secret is funny right up until you remember what's in the closet.

What I'm watching

  • Anyone official repeating the hack-to-"voter fraud" line, or saying a breached system held voter or citizenship data.

  • Whether Davies gets nominated, or just stays "acting" through Jan. 3.

  • The Federal Register, Dec. 18 through Dec. 21.

  • Three words next to "voter rolls": tainted, compromised, revalidate.

My tin-foil hat stays on through Dec. 18. If I'm wrong, I'll eat it. 🎩
