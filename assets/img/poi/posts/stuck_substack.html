Part two of two. Part one, Not SMART? Never Thought You Were, Duffy, covered every "not" about the ground stop. This one's about the "stuck."

"I'm stuck with L3Harris"

Here's the Transportation Secretary on Saturday, the day after the ground stop: "Was it the router, or were they not doing great work for us at L3Harris?" And: "It's our legacy telecom network. It's owned by L3Harris." And the line that gave this post its name: "until I can build that, I'm stuck with L3Harris, who owns this network."1

Stuck with them. Like a bad roommate on a lease you didn't sign.

Except the government signs these leases. Let's look at the ones it signed this year.

The cast

  • L3Harris Technologies, defense contractor, Melbourne, Florida. Runs the FAA's telecom network (FTI) since 2002, "99.99971% network reliability" by its own count.2 Fired its CEO in August for "conduct inconsistent with" its code of conduct.3
  • Trenchant, L3Harris's hacking-tools unit, built out of two companies it bought in 2018. Sells "exclusively to the U.S. government and select allied governments."4 Appears in L3Harris's annual report exactly once: in the list of subsidiaries.5
  • Peter Williams, Trenchant's former general manager. Pleaded guilty. More below.
  • Operation Zero, St. Petersburg. Buys exploits, promises to sell only to non-NATO customers. Sanctioned by Treasury in February.6
  • Coruna, an iPhone hacking kit. Google published it in March. It's been in Russian hands, Chinese hands, and as of Monday, on 178 big-brand websites.7
  • Sean Duffy, stuck.

Lease #1: through 2045

On July 1, L3Harris announced an FAA contract "to upgrade and continue to operate the nation's aircraft tracking network through 2045." That's the 700-plus ground stations feeding "real-time, satellite-based flight positioning data" to controllers. Its executive: "By expanding network capacity, we're ensuring the United States remains the global leader in air traffic management."8

So four months before Duffy said he was stuck with them, his agency extended them nineteen years. On a different network, sure. Same company, same "Mission Networks" division, whose income rose this year partly on "higher FAA volume."9

Lease #2: a billion dollars

In April, the Department of War agreed to buy 10,000 shares of preferred stock, at $100,000 a share, in Aerojet Rocketdyne, L3Harris's missile subsidiary. Net proceeds to L3Harris: $973 million. The company's own risk factors now warn investors about "unexpected issues related to the Department of War ('DoW') investment in our subsidiary."10

The government isn't stuck with L3Harris. The government is a shareholder. [P]

The part nobody's putting next to the FAA

Now the other L3Harris.

From 2022 to 2025, Trenchant's general manager sold at least eight of the company's hacking tools, made "for restricted use by the U.S. government and allied partners," to a Russian broker, under the name "John Taylor." One contract: $2 million for one tool, December 2023. When the company figured out in October 2024 that its products "had leaked," it put Williams in charge of the internal investigation. He blamed "a former employee." He delivered the eighth tool in July 2025, after the FBI was already on him.11

He confessed in August 2025, pleaded guilty to two counts of trade-secret theft (no espionage count, no export count), and got 87 months. Prosecutors said the sales "would have allowed the Russian Broker and its clients to potentially access millions of computers and devices around the world, including in the United States." The victim statements from "affected government entities" are under seal. Restitution to Trenchant and L3Harris was cut to $10 million from the $35 million prosecutors wanted; the damages hearing was canceled.12

Which tools? Sealed. But in March, two former L3Harris employees told TechCrunch that Coruna, the iPhone kit Google had just published, was "at least in part" developed by Trenchant. "Coruna was definitely an internal name of a component," one said. Google's own account of Coruna's travels: first seen with "a customer of a commercial surveillance vendor," then with "a suspected Russian espionage group" hitting Ukrainian sites, then with a Chinese crypto-theft crew, and "how it moved between these actors is unclear." TechCrunch noted the timeline "lines up" with Williams's sales. No official source has confirmed that.13

So: a U.S. government contractor's weapon, out in the world, passing through Russian and Chinese hands. [P] on the Williams-to-Coruna link; everything else above is on paper.

Monday

On November 16, somebody exploited a flaw in Optimizely, the website-testing tool, and planted malicious code that loaded on the sites of 178 organizations for about 17 hours. It skimmed cards, swapped crypto addresses, stole cookies, and, for iPhones running iOS 13 to 17, it served Coruna.14

The 178 include federal contractors: Salesforce (seven domains), Equifax and its income-verification service The Work Number, Zoom, GitLab.15 If you did your taxes, your benefits, or your background check through one of those this week, that's your phone the kit was looking at.

No federal advisory. The public warning came from a nonprofit.16

Same week, same patch

Meanwhile the NSA, which on October 21 told every federal agency the PHP bugs were "serious enough to require immediate patching across United States Government departments and agencies," and to take systems offline if they couldn't,17 took its own classified network down for three days applying that patch. A senior official told Fox it had never gone down in five years. NSA's statement called it "temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance."18 The DNI's version: patches "caused some of these systems to not come back up when you patch them as expected." "It's a good news story."19 It came back Friday.20

Remember who those bugs came through. OpenAI says it spent "the past two months" before October 21 running a coalition that briefed "the White House's Super Intelligence Force, the Office of the National Cyber Director (ONCD), the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)."21 The National Cyber Director resigned Thursday night.22

And on Thursday, Chase confirmed that hackers hit its vendors and posted thousands of customers' names, emails and some phone numbers. The bank says it's unrelated to Monday's outage (a "software bug") and unrelated to Optimizely.23 Maybe so. Nobody in government has said either way.

What this adds up to

I'm not telling you L3Harris hacked the FAA. I'm telling you what the paper says:

  1. The company that runs the network that failed got a new FAA contract to 2045 and a billion dollars from the Pentagon this year. [D]
  2. The same company's hacking unit lost its tools to Russia through its own general manager, and its iPhone kit is now hitting federal vendors' customers. [D, R]
  3. The same week, the government's own patch took down the NSA, and three financial-data incidents hit in five days. [D, R]
  4. Not one federal advisory connects any of it, or separates it. [D, by absence]

That last one is the point. We built a tripwire for this: a foreign-interference or compromised-system claim, then a "significant incident" declaration, then the FBI holds the evidence. The administration already has its ending written ("they know what the repercussions would be with me").24 What it's missing is a villain. An American-made weapon that's been through Russian and Chinese hands fits whichever label somebody picks. [P]

Stuck with them? The checks say sticking with them.
